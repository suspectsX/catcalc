Lilies and Cats: Why Even Pollen Is Dangerous

True lilies and daylilies can cause kidney failure in cats. Learn which plants, what to do after exposure, and safer flower choices.

By CatCalc editorial team · Updated 2026-11-02. General information only—not veterinary advice. We are not veterinarians.

Why this matters

Many owners bring home a bouquet without knowing that true lilies and daylilies are among the most dangerous household plants for cats. Unlike chocolate or onions, where a dose-related reaction is common, lily exposure in cats is treated as a veterinary emergency because kidney failure can follow even small amounts of pollen, leaf, or vase water.

CatCalc is not a clinic. This guide summarizes publicly available poison-control guidance so you can act quickly and choose safer flowers. Always follow your veterinarian's advice for your own cat.

Which plants are the problem

Poison-control resources commonly flag Lilium species (Easter, tiger, Asiatic, stargazer, and similar true lilies) and Hemerocallis (daylilies) as highly dangerous to cats. Other plants that people casually call "lilies" are not the same species—for example, peace lily and calla lily are different plants with different risk profiles—but they can still cause mouth and stomach irritation. When in doubt, treat an unlabeled "lily" bouquet as unsafe until a vet or poison hotline says otherwise.

Pollen brushed onto fur is a frequent exposure route: cats groom, then swallow pollen. Vase water can also be hazardous if a cat drinks from it. Keeping lilies in a closed room the cat never enters is not a reliable safeguard if pollen or leaves can travel.

Primary references: Pet Poison Helpline — lily, ASPCA Toxic and Non-Toxic Plants — Lily.

What to do if your cat may have been exposed

If you suspect contact with a true lily or daylily—chewing a leaf, licking pollen, drinking vase water, or walking through fallen stamens—contact a veterinarian or pet poison hotline right away, even if the cat looks fine. Early decontamination and monitoring matter; waiting for vomiting or lethargy can waste critical time.

  • In the US: ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center 888-426-4435 (fee may apply) or Pet Poison Helpline 855-764-7661.
  • Elsewhere: use your local emergency vet or national poison service for animals.
  • Bring the plant tag or a clear photo if you can identify the species.

Do not induce vomiting unless a veterinarian specifically instructs you to. Home remedies can make some poisonings worse.

Safer ways to enjoy flowers

If you want cut flowers in a home with cats, prefer varieties that poison-control lists treat as lower risk for cats, and still keep bouquets out of reach. Roses (watch thorns), sunflowers, orchids, and many herbs are commonly suggested as alternatives—but always double-check the specific plant, and avoid any bouquet that mixes in lily stems "for filler."

Artificial flowers remove the toxin risk entirely. If you receive a gift bouquet containing lilies, move it out of the home or strip those stems before the cat can investigate. Browse the ASPCA plant list before you buy.

FAQ

Are all plants with "lily" in the name equally toxic to cats?

No. True lilies (Lilium) and daylilies (Hemerocallis) are the classic high-risk group for kidney injury in cats. Other "lily" names refer to different plants. Identify the plant when possible, and ask a vet or poison hotline rather than guessing.

Is licking a small amount of pollen really an emergency?

Poison-control guidance treats exposure seriously because cats groom pollen off their fur. Contact a vet or hotline promptly; they will advise based on the plant and timing.

Can I keep lilies if my cat is never in that room?

Pollen and leaves can move on shoes, clothes, or drafts, and vase water can be accessible. The safest choice in a cat household is not to bring true lilies or daylilies inside.

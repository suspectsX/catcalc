New Kitten Checklist: Feeding, Safety, and Vet Timing

A practical first-weeks checklist for new kitten guardians: diet stage, household hazards, litter basics, and when to schedule veterinary care.

Updated 2026-11-02. General information only—not veterinary advice.

Before the kitten comes home

Set up a quiet room with a litter box, food and water bowls spaced apart, a scratching surface, and a hiding spot. Remove true lilies and daylilies from the home (see our lily guide). Cord protectors, secured windows, and closed laundry machines prevent common accidents.

Confirm the previous caregiver's diet and schedule so you can transition food gradually over about a week if you change brands. Abrupt swaps are a frequent cause of diarrhea in new homes.

Feeding life stage, not vibes

Growing kittens need a diet that meets growth (or all-life-stages) nutrient profiles, not an adult light formula. Free-choice dry food works for some households; measured meals help you notice appetite changes. Use the calorie calculator with the kitten factor only as a ballpark—kittens vary widely, and body condition beats any app.

Cow's milk is not kitten milk replacer. If a very young orphan needs bottle feeding, use a commercial kitten milk replacer and veterinary instructions—not dairy from your fridge (milk page).

Litter, socialization, and sleep

Most kittens take to a clean, uncovered box with unscented clumping or non-clumping litter. Scoop daily. Play with wand toys in short sessions; avoid using your hands as toys so biting habits do not form. Kittens sleep many hours—protect that rest from chaotic households.

Veterinary timing (general, not a schedule prescription)

New kittens typically need an exam soon after arrival for parasites, vaccine planning, FeLV/FIV testing discussions, and spay/neuter timing. Your clinic sets the exact schedule for your region and the kitten's age. Ask about microchipping and a recovery plan after surgery.

Keep a simple written log of weight, vaccines, and deworming—paper or a future printable planner—so the next appointment goes faster.

Foods and plants to keep away

Human foods that are unsafe for adult cats are unsafe for kittens too: chocolate, alcohol, onion family foods, raw dough, xylitol products, and cooked bones. Browse Can Cats Eat…? before sharing anything from your plate. String, rubber bands, and hair ties are choking and obstruction hazards that curious kittens love.

A calm first fortnight

Limit visitors for a few days if the kitten is shy. Keep dogs separated until supervised introductions are planned. Soft carriers for the first vet ride, a towel that smells like the previous home, and predictable meal times reduce stress more than fancy gadgets.

Write down baseline stool quality, appetite and weight after the first week. That notebook becomes invaluable if diarrhea or lethargy appears later. CatCalc tools support the boring-but-important parts—age stage, calories, food hazards—while your clinic handles vaccines and parasites.

FAQ

How soon should a new kitten see a vet?

Soon after arrival is typical so parasites, vaccines, and congenital issues can be checked. Ask your clinic for an appointment based on the kitten's age and history.

Can I feed an adult cat food to a kitten to save money?

Growth diets are formulated differently. Use a kitten or all-life-stages complete food unless your veterinarian directs otherwise.

How do I estimate kitten calories?

Use the calculator's kitten factor as a starting point, then adjust with frequent weigh-ins and body-condition checks guided by your vet.

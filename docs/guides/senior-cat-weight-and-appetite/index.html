Senior Cats: Tracking Weight and Appetite Changes

Why small weight swings matter in older cats, how to weigh at home, and when appetite changes deserve a veterinary visit—not a new treat flavor.

By CatCalc editorial team · Updated 2026-11-02. General information only—not veterinary advice. We are not veterinarians.

Senior is a life stage, not a vibe

The 2021 AAHA/AAFP feline life stage guidelines describe cats over 10 years as seniors (with mature adult roughly 7–10). Aging cats often hide discomfort. Gradual weight loss, drinking more, or skipping meals can be early clues for kidney disease, dental pain, hyperthyroidism, diabetes, or other conditions—problems a calculator cannot diagnose.

Source: AAHA/AAFP feline life stage definitions. CatCalc's age calculator maps life stages for orientation only.

A loss of even a small percentage of body weight can be meaningful in a cat; let your vet interpret the trend. Body-condition scoring (ribs easy to feel under a slight fat cover, visible waist from above in most cats) complements the scale.

Calories change with muscle and disease

Maintenance calorie estimates from RER × life-stage factors (see our calorie tool) assume a relatively typical adult. Seniors may need fewer calories if inactive, or more if they have conditions that raise metabolism. Weight-loss modes in any calculator are starting points for veterinary-supervised plans—not DIY crash diets.

Soft foods can help some cats with dental disease, but new diets for kidney or thyroid disease should be chosen with your vet. Do not crowd the bowl with tuna or milk as "appetite stimulants" without advice—those have their own limits on our food pages.

Appetite red flags

Seek veterinary care promptly if a senior cat stops eating for more than a day, drools, drops food, drinks and urinates much more than usual, hides constantly, or vomits repeatedly. Cats that do not eat are at risk of hepatic lipidosis; this is not a "wait and see" situation. Background reading: Cornell Feline Health Center — hepatic lipidosis.

What to bring to the vet

  • This weight/appetite log (even two weeks of numbers helps)
  • Current foods and treats with calorie statements if available
  • All medications and supplements with doses
  • Timeline: when symptoms started, whether weight fell, thirst rose, or litter habits changed
  • Prior lab results or clinic portal printouts if you have them
  • Questions written down (dental pain? kidney panel? thyroid? diet change?)

That preparation often matters more than any online article—including this one.

Environment tweaks that help older cats

Low-entry litter boxes, night lights in hallways, warmer resting spots and food bowls raised slightly can help arthritic or hypertensive seniors. None of these replace pain assessment or lab work, but they make daily life easier while you work with your vet.

If you use the CatCalc age tool and see “senior,” treat it as a calendar reminder to schedule wellness care—not as a reason to start random supplements from social media.

FAQ

At what age is a cat considered senior?

AAHA/AAFP life stage guidance commonly treats cats over 10 years as seniors, with mature adult around 7–10 years. Individual cats vary; ask your vet how often to examine older cats.

Is slow weight loss normal aging?

No. Unexplained weight loss deserves a workup. Do not dismiss it as "just getting old."

Should I put my senior cat on a weight-loss factor myself?

Only under veterinary guidance. Incorrect calorie restriction can worsen muscle loss or underlying disease.

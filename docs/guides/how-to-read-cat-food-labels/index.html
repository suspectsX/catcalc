How to Read a Cat Food Label (Without Marketing Spin)

A practical walkthrough of guaranteed analysis, calorie statements, ingredient lists, and AAFCO nutritional adequacy—so you can compare foods calmly.

By CatCalc editorial team · Updated 2026-11-02. General information only—not veterinary advice. We are not veterinarians.

Start with the job the food must do

Cat food packaging is designed to sell. Your job is narrower: find a complete diet that fits your cat's life stage, then use the label numbers to portion meals. Cats are obligate carnivores; they need nutrients found in animal tissue (including taurine) that plant-heavy or dog-food formulas may not supply in the right amounts.

This guide explains the parts of a label that matter for everyday feeding decisions. It does not rank brands or promise weight loss or disease outcomes. For broader feeding context see Cornell Feline Health Center — Feeding Your Cat and the WSAVA Global Nutrition Guidelines.

Nutritional adequacy statement (AAFCO language)

On many bags sold in the US you will see a statement that the food is formulated to meet AAFCO Cat Food Nutrient Profiles, or that it passed a feeding trial, for a named life stage (growth, adult maintenance, or all life stages). That statement is about completeness for the stated stage, not about taste, sustainability claims, or "human-grade" marketing.

Kitten growth and reproduction have higher nutrient targets than adult maintenance. An adult-maintenance food is generally not appropriate as the sole diet for growing kittens unless the label (and your vet) say otherwise. If your cat has a medical diet prescribed by a vet, follow that prescription over general label browsing.

Guaranteed analysis vs. calories

Guaranteed analysis lists minimums or maximums for crude protein, fat, fiber, and moisture. Those percentages are on an "as fed" basis unless a dry-matter conversion is provided. Wet food looks lower in protein percentage than dry food largely because of water—compare thoughtfully or ask your vet how to convert.

For portioning, the calorie statement (kcal per kg, per cup, or per can) is what our Cat Calorie Calculator expects. Manufacturers measure metabolizable energy; your calculator estimate is only a starting point. Adjust with body-condition scoring and veterinary guidance. AAHA nutrition and weight-management materials discuss energy needs and body condition in clinic settings: AAHA nutrition & weight management guidelines.

Worked example: dry-matter protein (demo data)

Example data only — not a real brand. Suppose a wet food label shows crude protein 10% (min) and moisture 78% (max) as-fed. Dry matter is roughly 100% − 78% = 22% of the can’s weight. Protein on a dry-matter basis ≈ 10 ÷ 22 × 100 ≈ 45%.

Suppose a dry food shows crude protein 32% (min) and moisture 10% (max). Dry matter ≈ 90%. Protein on a dry-matter basis ≈ 32 ÷ 90 × 100 ≈ 36%.

On the as-fed faceplate the wet food looked “lower protein” (10% vs 32%), but after removing water the wet example is actually higher on a dry-matter basis in this demo. Always use the numbers on your package; this arithmetic is a comparison aid, not a claim that one commercial diet is better.

After you finish comparing nutrients, enter the calorie statement into the calorie calculator to sketch a starting portion, then confirm with body-condition checks and your vet.

Ingredient list realities

Ingredients are listed by weight before cooking. Water-heavy items (fresh meat) can appear before meals even when meals contribute more dry matter after processing. "Meal" is not automatically bad; it is a concentrated ingredient. Buzzwords such as "holistic," "premium," or "ancestral" are not regulated nutrient standards.

Watch for onion or garlic powders in some toppers and broths—those belong in our onion and garlic hazard pages, not in a cat's bowl.

Treats and the 10% rule of thumb

Veterinary nutrition guidance commonly suggests keeping treats—including human-food bites—around or under about 10% of daily calories so the complete diet can still do its job. Enter treat calories into the same daily budget you use for meals. Our food safety list explains preparation limits; it is not a license to replace cat food with table scraps.

When to ask your veterinarian

Weight loss plans, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, food allergies, and recovery diets need individualized plans. Label literacy helps you ask better questions; it does not replace an exam or a prescription diet.

FAQ

Is grain-free automatically better for cats?

Not automatically. Cats need animal-sourced nutrients; "grain-free" is a marketing category, not a guarantee of higher quality. Discuss diet changes with your vet, especially if your cat has a medical condition.

Why does wet food show lower protein % than dry?

Moisture dilutes percentages on an as-fed basis. Compare products with dry-matter math or ask your vet; do not judge by the face-plate percentage alone.

Where do I find kcal per cup or per can?

Look for the calorie statement on the package or manufacturer site. Enter that number in the CatCalc calorie tool to estimate a starting portion.

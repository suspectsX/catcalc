Can cats eat coffee?

By CatCalc editorial team · Updated 2026-11-12 · Not veterinary advice

Do not feed

Caffeine can poison cats; keep coffee, grounds and beans away.

If your cat already ate this

  1. Contact your veterinarian or an emergency clinic now—do not wait for symptoms.
  2. In the US you can also call ASPCA Animal Poison Control: 888-426-4435 or Pet Poison Helpline: 855-764-7661 (fees may apply).
  3. Have ready: approximate amount and time, product packaging or ingredients, your cat’s weight, and any symptoms.
  4. Do not induce vomiting or give home remedies unless a veterinarian specifically instructs you to.

Why

Caffeine can poison cats. Coffee drinks, beans, grounds and caffeine-containing supplements differ substantially, so veterinary assessment should use the actual product and estimated amount.

Used grounds, tea bags, espresso products and energy supplements belong in the exposure history. A lick of a diluted drink and access to concentrated caffeine are not interchangeable events.

Identify the caffeine source before estimating the amount

The word coffee can mean a weak finished drink, an espresso concentrate, dry grounds or whole beans. Pet Poison Helpline explains that concentrated sources such as grounds, tea bags and supplements can present a more serious poisoning concern than a small lick of a beverage. Do not infer the amount of caffeine from the size or color of the spill alone.

When calling a veterinarian, name the brand and the exact product. Keep the container, serving information and any caffeine figure printed on it. If the drink came from a cafe, note the size and number of added shots if known. For an open packet of grounds, estimate the amount missing and explain whether the cat may have scattered some. A realistic range is better than claiming every missing gram was swallowed or dismissing it all as spilled material.

Milk and sweetness do not cancel the stimulant

Cornell's small-animal toxin reference identifies caffeine as toxic to cats and dogs. A milk-heavy coffee remains a caffeine-containing mixture. Cream, syrup or an iced presentation changes the product description; none is a way to make a coffee drink suitable for a cat. For the separate digestive limitations of dairy, see our milk guide.

A mocha can also contain chocolate, and a coffee cocktail brings in alcohol. Mention those ingredients during the same consultation. Do not focus on whichever ingredient sounds most familiar and leave out the others. If a sugar-free syrup was added, photograph its ingredient list. Cats do not necessarily share the dog response to every sweetener, so accurate identification is more useful than treating the phrase sugar-free as a complete toxicology answer.

Notice changes in activity without trying to diagnose them

According to Pet Poison Helpline, caffeine poisoning can involve unusual hyperactivity, digestive upset, changes in heart rate or rhythm, tremors and seizures. A restless cat is not simply getting a harmless energy boost. Report pacing, agitation, vomiting or shaking after a possible exposure and seek emergency care for seizures, collapse or trouble breathing.

Do not try to judge heart safety from a cat's apparent willingness to play. Some important changes need clinical assessment, and an owner watching at home cannot rule them out. If a veterinarian recommends observation, ask exactly which changes should trigger another call and where to obtain help overnight. Keep the original time of access in your notes; discovering a symptom later does not mean the cat must have found a second source of coffee.

The bin can matter as much as the mug

A morning coffee routine can leave grounds, filters, pods and stirrers in several places. Collect them directly into a closed waste container instead of leaving an open filter basket or used espresso puck on the counter. If a pod or bag is torn, tell the clinic whether pieces of its packaging are missing as well as its contents. Keep food-exposure details separate from possible swallowed objects.

Check backpacks and desk drawers for energy supplements or tablets. The poison-center caffeine resource lists concentrated energy products among the sources encountered in pets. Store them with the same care as other products the cat must not access. Decaffeinated labeling is not an invitation to offer the drink; there is no feline nutritional reason to make coffee part of the feeding routine, and the complete ingredient mix still requires attention after an accident.

Choose a different ritual for sharing breakfast

If your cat joins you at breakfast, place a portion of its usual food or another suitable reward in its own dish before opening the coffee supplies. This preserves a shared routine without letting the cat lick the mug, spoon or spilled drink. Teach everyone in the house the same rule, including guests who may think a creamy coffee is mostly milk.

The calorie calculator is useful for budgeting ordinary food and treats, but it cannot evaluate caffeine. Low sugar, few calories or a small serving size does not resolve a poisoning question. Our food-rating method separates toxic ingredients from routine feeding limits. After suspected caffeine ingestion, seek advice based on the product, amount and individual cat, rather than trying to fit the drink into a daily energy allowance or borrowing a human caffeine limit.

What to do

Call your vet if your cat drank or licked coffee grounds, beans or caffeine pills.

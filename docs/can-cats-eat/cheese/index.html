Can cats eat cheese?

By CatCalc editorial team · Updated 2026-11-12 · Not veterinary advice

Caution · fat and salt

Only a rare tiny piece if tolerated; cheese may cause digestive upset.

Why

Cheese is an optional dairy food that some cats tolerate poorly. Ingredients, calories, salt and lactose vary by product, so it is not a necessary or routine feline treat.

A lower lactose content does not prove tolerance, and no universal safe cheese portion has been established for every cat. Flavored spreads may add onion or garlic.

The cheese wrapper is a better guide than the cheese name

Cheese is a broad category, not a single feline snack. A slice, grated topping, soft spread and processed sauce can differ substantially in ingredients and calories. Before sharing anything, check the actual package and decide whether it contains a seasoning or flavor that changes the question. A product described as cheddar-flavored may include several ingredients besides cheese; a restaurant sauce may have no ingredient label available at all.

Dairy also has a tolerance problem independent of flavorings. ASPCA identifies milk and dairy as possible causes of diarrhea and digestive upset because pets have limited lactase. Some cheeses contain less lactose than milk, but that fact cannot predict an individual cat’s response. A cat that likes the smell is not demonstrating that its intestine will tolerate a serving.

Calcium does not make cheese a necessary supplement

It is easy to reason that cheese contains a useful nutrient, so adding cheese must improve the diet. Feline nutrition depends on amounts and proportions across the whole ration. Cornell notes that complete diets already supply required nutrients and that extra vitamins or minerals can be harmful. A cat eating an appropriate complete food does not need a cheese topping to make its bones stronger.

Neither should cheese be used to make an unbalanced homemade meat diet complete. Adding an arbitrary dairy serving does not establish the required mineral balance. If the main diet is homemade, a veterinarian with nutrition expertise should formulate it. See the cat-food label guide for distinguishing a food intended as a sole diet from a supplementary snack. Cheese is, at most, an optional extra for a cat whose own diet allows it.

A tiny reward still occupies the food budget

The practical difficulty with cheese is how easily small pieces accumulate. One person offers a nibble during breakfast, another uses it for a tablet, and a third sprinkles it over dinner. Each person may think the amount is trivial while nobody has counted the combined intake. Keep one household record for food rewards rather than separate private allowances.

WSAVA sets a maximum of 10% of daily calories from all treats together. Estimate daily energy with the calorie calculator, use the package’s energy information, and reserve room for every other treat. This is a calorie ceiling, not a validated safe cheese dose. A cat on a veterinary diet may need a different reward selected by the clinic even when a cheese piece fits the arithmetic.

Hiding a pill requires a medication check

Cheese is sometimes suggested as a convenient pill wrapper. Ask the prescribing clinic whether that specific medicine may be given with food and whether dairy is suitable. Do not assume that a technique suitable for one tablet applies to another, and do not crush or open medication unless instructed. A cat can also eat the wrapper and leave the tablet behind, so successful swallowing matters more than an empty treat bowl.

For a clinic-approved food reward, compare other options such as plain cooked chicken with dairy choices like yogurt. Selection should follow the cat’s treatment and dietary restrictions. Avoid onion- or garlic-flavored cheese spreads: ASPCA links Allium ingredients to red blood cell damage, with cats particularly susceptible. A flavored food cannot be made plain by scraping off the visible garnish.

If a clinic approves a particular cheese for a tablet, agree on one measured wrapper portion and who will give it. This prevents two family members from administering the same dose or each adding another unrecorded snack. Store the medication separately from the cheese package and record whether the tablet was swallowed, rather than merely whether food was offered.

After an accidental raid on the cheese plate

Put the remaining food away, identify what was actually eaten, and note whether wrappers or decorative picks are missing. A small plain nibble in a well cat and a large serving of an unknown spread need different discussions with a veterinarian. Do not label every episode of vomiting “lactose intolerance”; the timing alone cannot establish the cause.

Cornell lists lethargy, weakness, reduced appetite, blood and accompanying diarrhea as reasons for prompt veterinary assessment of vomiting. Supply the label or recipe and the approximate amount. If digestive upset follows dairy repeatedly, stop offering it rather than searching for a slightly different cheese that will permit the habit. Choosing a reward the cat tolerates, or using part of its regular food allowance, is simpler than managing a recurring food experiment.

If offering cheese

Avoid routine cheese rewards. Ask the prescribing clinic before hiding medication in cheese or another food.

Treats and complete diets

If this food is appropriate at all, keep it inside the daily calorie budget. Use the calorie calculator and keep treats near or under about 10% of total kcal. Human food is not a balanced cat diet.

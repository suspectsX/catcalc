Can cats eat raw fish?

By CatCalc editorial team · Updated 2026-11-12 · Not veterinary advice

Cook first

Do not serve raw fish. Cook it plainly and remove bones before sharing.

Why

Some raw fish contain thiaminases that can deplete vitamin B1 when fed repeatedly, and raw animal foods can transmit pathogens. Cooking does not make a fish-only diet nutritionally complete.

Thiaminase content varies among fish. A single exposure and a long-term raw-fish diet are different situations; neither warrants an invented home vitamin dose.

Two risks that should not be collapsed into one

Raw fish raises both food-safety and nutritional questions. Germs can be present in uncooked animal foods, while particular fish can also contain enzymes that interfere with nutrients. These are different mechanisms: a product might look fresh and still carry a food-safety risk, and repeated use of an unsuitable raw fish diet can create a nutritional problem without an obvious spoiled smell.

Cornell does not recommend raw diets because they can carry bacteria, viruses and parasites without a demonstrated clear health advantage. This concern extends to people who handle the food or touch contaminated bowls and surfaces. Keeping a raw-fish portion separate from human dinner does not remove the exposure pathway. Choose a cooked, boneless preparation instead of treating a pet-food label or a sushi counter as proof that raw fish is appropriate for cats.

What thiaminase actually means

Thiamine is vitamin B1. Thiaminases are enzymes in some raw fish that break it down, which can matter when those foods are fed repeatedly as a substantial part of the diet. Merck Veterinary Manual describes thiaminases in raw freshwater fish and clams, their destruction by cooking, and neurological signs of thiamine deficiency in cats. The mechanism is not a claim that all fish species contain equal amounts or that one lick inevitably causes deficiency.

It also does not justify adding an arbitrary vitamin tablet to raw fish. A supplement selected at home cannot certify that the whole diet is complete or address microbial hazards. If raw fish has become a regular meal, tell your veterinarian the species, amount, frequency and duration. Include any supplements already used so that the diet can be assessed as a whole.

A useful history includes whether the fish was a small reward, a topper or the main meal. “Sometimes” and “a little” can mean very different things in different homes. Estimate the number of meals per week and the duration of the habit, and name the fish when possible. Those concrete details help the clinic assess dietary exposure without guessing.

Freezing and marinating do not create a complete safety plan

A household freezer is a storage tool; it should not be treated as evidence that every possible pathogen has been eliminated. Likewise, citrus marinade, salting, drying or the words “human grade” do not convert a raw animal product into a complete feline ration. Different processes address different hazards, and you should not infer a universal kill step from a kitchen technique.

CDC recommends against raw pet food and treats because of germs such as Salmonella and Listeria. Wash hands, utensils and food-contact surfaces after handling raw fish, and keep the cat away during preparation. Do not offer trimmings while waiting for the main portion to cook. A cooked fish reward is a separate decision, explained on the cooked-salmon page and tuna page.

How cooking changes the decision

Cook plain fish before adding human sauces, then inspect it carefully for bones and let the cat’s small portion cool. FDA’s food-handling guidance gives 145°F for fin fish, or opaque flesh that separates easily with a fork. These are household food-safety instructions, not a veterinary prescription or a guarantee that every prepared dish is suitable. Garlic butter, onion sauce and salty marinades remain separate problems after cooking.

Cooked fish still needs a dietary role. WSAVA advises that all treats together contribute no more than 10% of a cat’s daily calories. Use the calorie calculator to understand the day’s total, and use complete cat food for the main ration. Cooking destroys thiaminase but does not, by itself, add all the nutrients missing from a fish-only homemade menu.

What to tell the clinic after exposure

For a stolen raw-fish piece, record the time, approximate amount, fish type if known, seasoning and whether bones were present. For habitual feeding, describe the full menu rather than only the most recent bite. These details help the veterinarian decide which exposure or nutritional issues need attention. Do not induce vomiting or start a vitamin treatment from an internet dose chart.

Loss of coordination, seizures or marked weakness need urgent veterinary assessment; Merck includes neurological dysfunction among feline thiamine-deficiency signs. Repeated vomiting, reduced appetite, lethargy or accompanying diarrhea also warrant advice, as outlined by Cornell. Symptoms cannot establish thiamine deficiency at home, and the absence of symptoms does not validate continuing the same raw diet. The cat-food label guide can help prepare a more suitable long-term feeding discussion.

Cook before serving

Do not offer raw fish. If sharing fish, cook it plainly, inspect for bones and keep it an occasional extra within the cat's feeding plan.

Treats and complete diets

If this food is appropriate at all, keep it inside the daily calorie budget. Use the calorie calculator and keep treats near or under about 10% of total kcal. Human food is not a balanced cat diet.

Can cats eat chives?

By CatCalc editorial team · Updated 2026-11-12 · Not veterinary advice

Do not feed

Chives and other Alliums can damage cats' red blood cells.

If your cat already ate this

  1. Contact your veterinarian or an emergency clinic now—do not wait for symptoms.
  2. In the US you can also call ASPCA Animal Poison Control: 888-426-4435 or Pet Poison Helpline: 855-764-7661 (fees may apply).
  3. Have ready: approximate amount and time, product packaging or ingredients, your cat’s weight, and any symptoms.
  4. Do not induce vomiting or give home remedies unless a veterinarian specifically instructs you to.

Why

Chives are Allium plants that can damage cats' red blood cells. Their use as a small garnish does not make intentional feeding appropriate.

Fresh chives in an herb pot, chopped garnish and dried chives in a seasoning jar all need to be identified accurately after an exposure.

A kitchen herb is still an Allium

Chives look very different from a bulb onion, but the relevant question is the plant family. ASPCA identifies chives, Allium schoenoprasum, as toxic to cats. Its clinical summary includes red blood cell breakdown as well as digestive signs. The green blades should therefore not be offered as a fresh herb snack or mixed into cat food.

Do not confuse an indoor herb pot with cat grass simply because both present narrow green leaves. Keep the plant label when buying kitchen herbs and place chives where the cat cannot chew them. If an unidentified plant was nibbled, photograph the whole plant and the label rather than supplying a guess based on leaf shape. A reliable identification helps the veterinary team choose the relevant advice without treating every grass-like plant as the same exposure.

The garnish is part of the finished dish

Chives are often added after cooking, which makes them easy to overlook when someone describes a meal as plain. An omelet, soft cheese, potato topping or soup may contain chopped chives even if the main ingredient is otherwise familiar. Ask about garnish as well as seasoning when checking what a cat ate.

Our cooked-egg page concerns eggs prepared without these extras. The cheese page likewise does not give permission to share a flavored chive spread. For an accidental exposure, save the tub or recipe and estimate the amount of the whole dish eaten. Do not spend time sorting green flecks from a plate to calculate a supposedly safe count. Report the uncertainty and let a veterinarian assess the combination of ingredients and the cat's circumstances.

Why drying or cooking is not a clearance test

Merck's Allium review includes chives in the same group as onions and garlic and explains that processed Allium foods can remain a concern. A dried herb jar belongs in the ingredient check; a cooked topping should not be assumed harmless because the green pieces have softened. There is no established household preparation that turns chives into a recommended feline treat.

This is different from the raw-versus-cooked decision for a food such as an egg. Cooking can address certain food-handling hazards, but it does not create a universal rule that every cooked ingredient is safe. When several risks share a plate, address all of them. For instance, identifying a plain cooked base does not clear a dish after chives have been stirred through it. Set aside any suitable cat portion before adding the herb.

Signs are not limited to an upset stomach

The ASPCA chive entry lists weakness, blood in urine, a high heart rate and panting among possible signs, along with vomiting and hemolytic anemia. Seek prompt veterinary help for concerning changes after an exposure, and use emergency care for collapse or trouble breathing. A symptom list is a reason to get help, not a home method for confirming the cause.

Give the clinic the time chives were available and the time any change was first seen. Include other Allium exposure, such as onion in the same soup or garlic in the spread. If the cat has been grazing on an herb pot for an unknown period, say so. The history should distinguish a witnessed single bite from repeated access rather than forcing both situations into an exact amount.

Manage herb pots and meal preparation together

A shelf of cooking herbs and a plate of leftovers are two separate access points. Move a chive pot into a closed room or another place the cat cannot enter; do not rely only on moving it a little higher. During meal preparation, collect trimmings directly into a closed container instead of leaving a small pile on the counter.

Before serving guests, explain that green garnish counts when deciding whether food can be shared. Provide a bowl with the cat's usual food if someone wants to give a reward. After eating, clear chive-smeared plates and utensils before the cat investigates them. Our food-rating guide explains why ingredient identification comes before calories. A garnish may contribute little energy to a human meal while still being an ingredient that should not be intentionally offered to a cat.

What to do

Do not offer chives or chive-seasoned foods. If your cat ate them, contact your veterinarian with the amount, timing and complete dish or plant description.

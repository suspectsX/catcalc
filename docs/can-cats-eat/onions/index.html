Can cats eat onions?

By CatCalc editorial team · Updated 2026-11-12 · Not veterinary advice

Do not feed

Onions can damage cats' red blood cells, including cooked or powdered onion.

If your cat already ate this

  1. Contact your veterinarian or an emergency clinic now—do not wait for symptoms.
  2. In the US you can also call ASPCA Animal Poison Control: 888-426-4435 or Pet Poison Helpline: 855-764-7661 (fees may apply).
  3. Have ready: approximate amount and time, product packaging or ingredients, your cat’s weight, and any symptoms.
  4. Do not induce vomiting or give home remedies unless a veterinarian specifically instructs you to.

Why

Onions can cause oxidative injury to cats' red blood cells and hemolytic anemia. Raw, cooked, dehydrated and powdered onions remain a concern; signs of anemia can be delayed after ingestion.

Onion can be part of a recipe even when no visible onion pieces remain. Check soup bases, gravies, seasoning mixtures and foods offered to hide medication.

The problem is red blood cell injury

Onion poisoning is different from the digestive upset that can follow an unfamiliar meal. ASPCA's onion entry identifies cats as a susceptible species and lists destruction of red blood cells among the effects. The resulting anemia reduces the blood's ability to carry oxygen. It is therefore misleading to judge an onion-containing meal only by whether the cat vomited soon afterward.

The Merck Allium review explains that concerning exposures include raw, cooked, dehydrated and granulated onion. Cooking a sauce until the onion disappears does not remove the underlying concern. Keep that distinction in mind when a cat eats a spoonful of gravy or soup rather than a recognizable onion ring. What matters for the consultation is the ingredient history, not whether the original vegetable can still be picked out of the dish.

Broth and baby food need the same ingredient check as leftovers

Broth sometimes becomes an improvised way to tempt a cat that is not eating. Before offering any human broth, inspect the entire ingredient list. Onion, onion powder and onion-containing soup concentrates can be less obvious than a pile of chopped onion. The garlic page covers another Allium that may appear in the same product; a product without onion can still contain garlic.

Do the same check for baby food or a soft food intended to hide a tablet. Ask the prescribing veterinarian what can be used with that medicine rather than assuming a smooth meat puree is suitable. Save the container if the food has already been eaten. Homemade broth needs a recipe review too: straining out solids does not turn a seasoned stock into the plain meat discussed on our cooked-chicken page.

Small repeated offerings belong in the history

An exposure history is more useful when it includes the whole recent feeding routine. If someone has been adding a little soup to meals, record how often, approximately how much, and which soup it was. Do not report only the most recent spoonful because earlier offerings seem irrelevant. ASPCApro's feline toxic-exposure guidance discusses onion-containing food as a specific concern in cats.

There is no household measuring trick that reliably turns a mixed recipe into a safe feline onion allowance. A teaspoon of powder and a teaspoon of cooked onion are not interchangeable ingredient descriptions. Avoid deciding that a powder is harmless because its volume is small. Give the veterinary team the best estimate available and explain what cannot be measured. That is more useful than making a confident but unsupported calculation from an online toxic-dose table.

Delayed weakness is a reason to reconnect with the clinic

ASPCA lists weakness, blood in the urine, an increased heart rate and panting among onion-related signs. Merck also describes delayed anemia after Allium ingestion. Contact the clinic promptly about pale gums, unusual weakness, collapse or breathing changes. These observations require professional assessment; they do not prove onion poisoning or exclude another illness.

Ask the veterinarian whether rechecks or monitoring are needed and follow the plan for that exposure. An uneventful first evening does not, by itself, answer the later blood-cell question. Keep the product packaging and the original timeline until the team says they are no longer needed. If different people care for the cat, write down who is responsible for observing meals and behavior so a change is not missed during a shift between family members.

A practical rule for shared meals

Set aside any intended cat treat before onion or other seasoning enters the pan. Once a dish contains onion, do not offer a supposedly clean piece from the same sauce. For garnish-heavy dishes, serve the cat's appropriate food in a separate bowl before the human meal begins; this reduces the chance of someone sharing from a finished plate.

The same kitchen rule applies to chives and garlic, although those ingredients deserve their own labels when reporting an accident. Teach visitors the ingredient names instead of merely saying the cat cannot have spicy food: onion need not taste hot. Our ratings guide explains why this is a poisoning category rather than a portion-control issue. A daily calorie budget never creates an allowance for onion, and it should not be used to justify a trial serving.

What to do

Contact your vet. Signs such as weakness, pale gums or red-brown urine can appear days later.

Can cats eat tuna?

By CatCalc editorial team · Updated 2026-11-02 · Not veterinary advice

Caution · treat only

Plain tuna in spring water is an occasional treat, never a complete diet.

Why

Cats love tuna, but human canned tuna is not a complete diet and a tuna-heavy diet has been linked to nutritional problems. Tuna packed in brine is also salty.

A tuna-only diet can contribute to nutritional imbalance over time. Human canned tuna in brine is salty; oil-packed tuna adds fat. Fish bones in some products are a separate injury risk. Rotate complete cat foods as the staple; keep tuna as a high-value training bit in spring water when you use it at all.

Spring water vs brine or oil

Prefer plain tuna in spring water as an occasional high-value bit. Brine adds salt; oil adds fat. Seasoned pouches with broth may hide onion or garlic powder—read labels.

Treat vs staple diet

Cats may prefer tuna to balanced cat food. Making tuna the main diet risks nutritional gaps over time. Complete commercial cat food (or a vet-designed home-prepared diet) should remain the staple.

What to tell the vet

If a tuna binge led to vomiting, diarrhea or refusal of normal food, say how much tuna, which packing liquid, and how long tuna has been a large share of calories.

How to serve it

Offer tuna in spring water only as an occasional treat, not a meal replacement.

Treats and complete diets

If this food is appropriate at all, keep it inside the daily calorie budget. Use the calorie calculator and keep treats near or under about 10% of total kcal. Human food is not a balanced cat diet.

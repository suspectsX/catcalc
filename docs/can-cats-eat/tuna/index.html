Can cats eat tuna?

By CatCalc editorial team · Updated 2026-11-12 · Not veterinary advice

Caution · treat only

Plain tuna in spring water is an occasional treat, never a complete diet.

Why

Human canned tuna is not a complete cat diet. Plain tuna may be an occasional reward if appropriate for the cat, but the packing liquid, seasoning and overall diet matter.

Check the nutritional-adequacy statement on feline tuna foods rather than assuming that every fish product is complete. A cat eating only human tuna needs a diet review.

A tuna can and a complete tuna cat food have different jobs

The fish pictured on two containers can be the same while their nutritional roles are very different. Human canned tuna is a food ingredient. A feline diet that contains tuna may also include the vitamins, minerals and other nutrients needed for a complete ration. The decisive information is the nutritional-adequacy statement, not the fish name or how strongly your cat wants to eat it.

FDA explains that a complete-and-balanced claim identifies food intended as a sole diet, while treats and supplements generally do not serve that role. Use the cat-food label guide to locate that statement. A cat that eats tuna enthusiastically is demonstrating preference, not confirming that a tuna-only menu will supply everything it needs. Do not replace a growing portion of complete food with plain fish simply because the bowl is emptied faster.

A pouch sold in the pet aisle can also be supplementary rather than complete. Do not assume that location in the shop settles the question. Keep a photograph of the adequacy statement alongside the product name when asking the clinic about a new food; that makes it easier to distinguish the intended staple from a topper with a similar label.

The packing liquid and recipe matter

Choose a plain product if the veterinary team allows tuna as a reward. Spring water is easier to assess than oil, brine, flavored sauce or a prepared tuna salad, but still read the label: “in water” does not necessarily mean that nothing else was added. Seasoned pouches and ready-made sandwich fillings deserve the same ingredient review as any human mixed dish.

Onion and garlic are particularly relevant to savory tuna preparations. ASPCA identifies Allium ingredients as a cause of red blood cell injury in cats. Taking visible onion pieces out of tuna salad does not remove the rest of the recipe. If an unsafe ingredient was eaten, use the onion page or garlic page to prepare information for the veterinarian rather than treating the exposure as ordinary tuna.

Fish-only feeding is not a shortcut to better nutrition

Tuna is often promoted as a natural alternative for a finicky cat. The important distinction is between a limited reward and an unsupervised long-term diet. Cornell cautions that some cats eating canned fish products intended for humans have developed serious neurological problems. This does not mean that one plain flake produces that outcome; it means that a human fish product should not be assumed to be a balanced feline staple.

Avoid adding supplements from several bottles to “fix” a tuna-only ration yourself. Feline diet formulation requires correct amounts and proportions, and an improvised addition may create a new imbalance. If your cat accepts nothing except tuna, arrange a veterinary appointment and bring a detailed diet history. The senior weight and appetite guide is useful when this change accompanies weight loss or a newly selective appetite.

Use the aroma carefully, and keep track of the calories

A tiny amount of a clinic-approved tuna product may be useful as a reward, but repeatedly covering meals with it can make the day’s diet difficult to measure. Separate the reward portion before opening the cat’s regular food, record it, and ensure that every person in the home uses the same allowance. The liquid itself is not automatically calorie-free or appropriate for a medical diet.

WSAVA recommends limiting all treats to no more than 10% of daily energy. The calorie calculator can estimate a starting daily target; your veterinarian may choose a different figure for the individual cat. Calculate from the actual product’s label rather than a generic spoonful. A numerical budget is an upper limit for extras, not a recommendation that tuna be eaten every day or that a particular serving is safe for every medical condition.

Cooked, raw and spoiled fish are separate questions

This page concerns plain canned tuna as an optional treat, not raw sushi fish or an aging open can. CDC advises safe handling of pet food and warns against raw animal foods because of harmful germs. Keep opened food refrigerated as directed, use clean utensils, and do not use smell alone to establish that a forgotten portion is safe. See raw-fish risks for why raw fish is not equivalent to the canned version.

If vomiting is repeated or accompanied by reduced appetite, weakness, lethargy, blood or diarrhea, seek veterinary advice using Cornell’s symptom guidance. Bring the brand and ingredients, not just the word “tuna.” Persistent refusal of complete food deserves attention even when the cat is still willing to eat its favorite fish.

How to serve it

If your veterinarian allows it, reserve plain tuna for a small occasional reward and continue a complete feline diet.

Treats and complete diets

If this food is appropriate at all, keep it inside the daily calorie budget. Use the calorie calculator and keep treats near or under about 10% of total kcal. Human food is not a balanced cat diet.

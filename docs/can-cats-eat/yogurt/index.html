Can cats eat yogurt?

By CatCalc editorial team · Updated 2026-11-12 · Not veterinary advice

Caution · plain only

A small amount of plain unsweetened yogurt may be tolerated; check ingredients.

Why

Yogurt is an optional dairy food, and lactose or other ingredients may cause digestive upset. A live-culture claim does not establish a treatment benefit for cats.

Plain, unsweetened labeling does not guarantee tolerance. Check all ingredients; yogurt is not a substitute for a feline probiotic chosen by your veterinary team.

A live-culture label is not a treatment plan

A yogurt pot can advertise live cultures without demonstrating that the product will treat a cat’s digestive disease. The name of an ingredient is not evidence for a clinical benefit in a particular species, at a particular dose, for a particular condition. If a veterinarian recommends a probiotic, ask which product and how to judge the response rather than substituting a human breakfast food.

Yogurt remains a dairy food. ASPCA warns that milk and other dairy products can cause digestive upset when lactose is poorly digested. Fermentation does not justify assuming that every yogurt contains no lactose or that every cat can tolerate it. A cat with ongoing diarrhea needs an assessment of the problem; adding more yogurt can make it harder to know which dietary change was responsible for the next symptom.

Plain means checking the whole container

Look past the fruit pictured on the lid. Fruit preparations, flavored syrups, chocolate pieces, granola and sweeteners turn a pot into a mixture, and some ingredients may be packaged in a separate compartment. Do not assume that a spoonful taken from the white part has an ingredient list different from the combined product. If you cannot identify the ingredients, choose a reward already approved for the cat instead.

Read chocolate guidance for cocoa-containing products and xylitol guidance for the important distinction between feline and canine evidence. ASPCApro describes gastrointestinal upset from xylitol in cats; the well-known dog poisoning pattern should not be automatically asserted for cats. An unknown sugar-free mixture still warrants a call with the actual label, especially if other hazardous ingredients are involved.

Keep the lid and the outer sleeve until you know which product was eaten; the reusable pot alone may not carry the complete ingredient list. If multiple pots are open in a shared fridge, label the one intended for a clinic-approved food task. This avoids confusing a plain product with an almost identical flavored or sweetened version.

Greek, lactose-free and plant-based are different claims

“Greek” describes a style of yogurt, not proof of suitability for a cat. “Lactose-free” addresses one sugar, not whether a product is complete feline nutrition. A plant-based yogurt is not dairy, but it may contain its own flavorings and sweeteners; the absence of cow’s milk does not certify the rest of the recipe. None of these labels creates a reason to supplement a complete cat diet.

Cornell explains that cats require a carefully balanced supply of nutrients and generally do not need supplements alongside a complete diet. Compare the role of yogurt with milk and cheese rather than arranging dairy foods into a universal safe-to-unsafe ladder. An individual history of poor tolerance is more useful than a marketing category.

How an optional taste fits into a real day

If your veterinary team agrees that a particular plain product is suitable, treat any taste as part of the food budget. Do not begin with a standard teaspoon for every cat: spoon sizes, product energy density, medical needs and tolerance vary. An amount that contributes few calories can still cause trouble for a sensitive cat, so a calorie calculation cannot be used as a tolerance test.

WSAVA recommends no more than 10% of daily calories from treats. Add yogurt to the same tally as biscuits and training rewards, using the calorie calculator only as a starting estimate for the daily total. Our cat-food label guide explains the difference between supplementary foods and complete diets. If the cat refuses its normal meal but eagerly eats yogurt, contact the clinic about appetite rather than letting yogurt become the replacement meal.

What to record if the next litter-box visit is abnormal

Stop the new food and write down the product, approximate amount, time and other recent changes. Keep the container for its ingredient list. Avoid offering several different dairy products in succession; that creates a confusing food history rather than a controlled answer to whether yogurt was tolerated.

Repeated vomiting, blood, weakness, lethargy or a reduction in appetite should prompt veterinary advice, as described in Cornell’s vomiting guidance. Be especially clear if a prescribed diet or treatment was already in progress. Yogurt cannot diagnose food intolerance, replace fluid treatment, or establish that the intestine has recovered. For an older cat with appetite or weight changes, the senior weight and appetite guide can help organize observations for the appointment without turning a yogurt preference into a diagnosis.

If offering yogurt

Do not use yogurt to treat diarrhea. Ask your veterinarian before adding it, especially for a cat on a prescribed diet.

Treats and complete diets

If this food is appropriate at all, keep it inside the daily calorie budget. Use the calorie calculator and keep treats near or under about 10% of total kcal. Human food is not a balanced cat diet.

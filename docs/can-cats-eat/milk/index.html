Can cats eat milk?

By CatCalc editorial team · Updated 2026-11-12 · Not veterinary advice

Caution · lactose

Cow's milk often causes digestive upset; it is not a kitten milk replacement.

Why

Many cats digest lactose poorly, and cow's milk can cause diarrhea or other digestive upset. Milk is not a necessary addition to a complete feline diet.

Lactose-free milk can still be an incomplete, calorie-containing treat. Pasteurization reduces microbial risk but does not correct lactose intolerance or establish nutritional completeness.

Why the saucer tradition is misleading

A cat may eagerly lap milk without digesting it comfortably. Lactose is the sugar in milk; lactase is the intestinal enzyme needed to break it down. ASPCA explains that limited lactase activity can allow dairy to cause diarrhea and digestive upset. Enjoying the taste therefore tells you little about tolerance. A cat that seemed comfortable after one stolen lick has not passed a lifelong milk test, and a daily saucer is not an appropriate reward routine.

This is different from saying that every trace of milk is a veterinary emergency. If an otherwise well adult cat steals a lick of plain pasteurized milk, remove the saucer and watch its behavior and litter box. Describe the actual product if you call the clinic: cow’s milk, cream, a sweetened drink and a coffee creamer are not interchangeable exposures.

An orphaned kitten needs a different product

The familiar picture of a kitten drinking cow’s milk confuses two nutritional situations. An unweaned kitten needs an appropriate milk-replacement feeding plan, whereas an adult cat should receive a complete diet for its life stage. Cornell describes the many nutrients that feline diets must supply; simply supplying white liquid does not meet that standard. Contact a veterinarian or an experienced rescue working with a veterinarian for an orphan’s formula, feeding method, frequency and growth checks.

Do not use lactose-free human milk, diluted cream, evaporated milk or a homemade kitchen mixture as a replacement selected from this article. Removing lactose changes one ingredient; it does not establish the correct balance for growth. Our kitten checklist is an organizing aid for arranging care, rather than a hand-rearing recipe.

Lactose-free does not mean unrestricted

A product sold as cat milk may have reduced lactose, but read whether it is a complementary treat or a complete food. Its calories still belong in the day’s total, and its label does not make it a required supplement. The cat-food label guide explains why a nutrient claim on the front of a package is less useful than the nutritional-adequacy statement and feeding information.

WSAVA recommends that treats contribute no more than 10% of daily calories, with additional restrictions possible for medical diets. That ceiling covers all extras together, including milk, lickable treats and pieces used for medication. Use the calorie calculator as a starting estimate, then follow your own veterinarian’s target. There is no reason to spend that allowance on a drink that repeatedly causes loose stools.

For a household that already buys a cat-milk treat, write the opened date on the container and follow the manufacturer’s refrigeration and use instructions. Do not top an old saucer up with fresh milk. Measuring a portion into a clean dish makes both its intake and any change in tolerance easier to describe accurately to the veterinary team.

Milk drinks bring their own ingredient list

Chocolate milk is a chocolate exposure as well as a dairy exposure. Coffee drinks can contain caffeine; flavored creamers can contain several ingredients that are absent from plain milk. Read the container rather than assuming that the pale color makes a drink harmless. The chocolate page and coffee page explain why those ingredients require prompt professional advice.

Raw milk is a separate food-safety concern. CDC advises against raw pet food and unpasteurized milk because they can carry harmful germs. Pasteurization addresses a microbial hazard; it does not remove lactose or turn milk into balanced cat food. Keep drinking glasses out of reach and use fresh water as the normal drink. If a cat consistently prefers milk while avoiding water or regular meals, discuss the underlying feeding problem rather than increasing the saucer.

When the problem is more than a messy litter box

Stop offering milk if it is followed by digestive upset. Record the quantity, time, product and any other recent diet change; that history helps the clinic distinguish an exposure from an unrelated illness. Do not keep challenging a symptomatic cat with progressively smaller portions to prove that dairy is responsible.

Cornell advises veterinary evaluation for vomiting accompanied by weakness, lethargy, reduced appetite, blood or diarrhea. A kitten, an older cat with weight loss, or a cat already receiving treatment deserves advice suited to its circumstances. Use our senior weight and appetite guide to prepare observations for an appointment. Milk does not treat dehydration or restore appetite safely by itself, and an online article cannot determine how much fluid a sick cat needs.

Milk alternatives

Avoid routine milk feeding. For an unweaned or orphaned kitten, ask a veterinarian for an appropriate milk-replacer and feeding plan.

Treats and complete diets

If this food is appropriate at all, keep it inside the daily calorie budget. Use the calorie calculator and keep treats near or under about 10% of total kcal. Human food is not a balanced cat diet.

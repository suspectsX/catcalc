Can cats eat alcohol?

By CatCalc editorial team · Updated 2026-11-12 · Not veterinary advice

Do not feed

Alcohol can cause serious poisoning; ingestion requires urgent veterinary advice.

If your cat already ate this

  1. Contact your veterinarian or an emergency clinic now—do not wait for symptoms.
  2. In the US you can also call ASPCA Animal Poison Control: 888-426-4435 or Pet Poison Helpline: 855-764-7661 (fees may apply).
  3. Have ready: approximate amount and time, product packaging or ingredients, your cat’s weight, and any symptoms.
  4. Do not induce vomiting or give home remedies unless a veterinarian specifically instructs you to.

Why

Alcohol can cause serious poisoning in cats. Ethanol can depress the nervous system and disrupt normal body function; a suspected ingestion warrants prompt veterinary advice.

Give the clinic the exact beverage or product name. Beer, spirits, alcohol-filled sweets and fermenting yeast dough are different descriptions, and a mixed drink may contain additional concerning ingredients.

A spill is an exposure, even when nobody offered a drink

Alcohol accidents are not limited to someone deliberately giving a cat beer. An unattended glass, a tipped cocktail or a liquor-filled sweet can create access. Pet Poison Helpline identifies alcoholic drinks, liquor-containing candies and unbaked yeast dough as potential sources of ethanol exposure. Record what the cat actually reached rather than describing the event only as a kitchen accident.

For a beverage, keep the bottle or a photograph showing its name and alcohol concentration. For a cocktail, list the spirits, mixer and any extra flavorings. If the amount is unknown because a glass spilled, estimate how much was present before and after without claiming that everything missing was swallowed. Say clearly whether the cat was seen drinking, was found beside the spill, or had liquid on its coat. Those are different observations for the veterinary team.

Alcohol affects more than coordination

The MSD Veterinary Manual alcohol review describes rapid absorption and effects including nervous-system depression, reduced body temperature and metabolic disturbance. A cat staggering after an exposure should not be treated as an amusing version of human drunkenness. Severe intoxication can impair breathing and become life-threatening.

Seek veterinary help promptly for suspected ingestion, and emergency care if there is difficulty breathing, collapse, seizures or marked inability to respond normally. Vomiting and poor coordination also deserve immediate reporting. Do not wait for every symptom on a list to appear. A list describes possible effects, not a checklist that must be completed before the situation counts as serious. Record the time each observed sign started so the clinic can relate changes to the possible exposure and decide what care is needed.

Mixed drinks need a complete ingredient description

A coffee cocktail is both an alcohol question and a caffeine question. A chocolate liqueur also belongs in the chocolate discussion. These ingredients should be mentioned together when asking for advice, rather than making separate assumptions that each amount seems too small to matter. Keep a recipe or bar receipt if it helps identify what was served.

Human calorie labels and sugar-free claims do not answer the alcohol question. Likewise, a creamy texture does not make a drink equivalent to the milk discussed on its own page. For cooking sauces or desserts, describe the recipe and preparation as accurately as possible instead of deciding from taste that all alcohol must be gone. When the amount remaining is uncertain, tell the clinician that; an unsupported estimate should not become the basis for home reassurance.

Not every product called alcohol is a beverage

MSD distinguishes ethanol from other alcohols found in household products, including methanol and isopropanol. The product name therefore matters. A cat licking a cleaning liquid should not be described as simply having had a sip of alcohol; bring the container so its ingredients can be identified. Tell the clinic about skin or coat contact as well as anything swallowed.

This page is not a cleaning-product treatment guide. Do not apply another household chemical, give a home antidote or improvise care from a human first-aid recipe. Ask the veterinary team how to manage the actual exposure while arranging care. Keeping the original container upright and away from the cat also prevents another exposure during the call. If there are other pets, check where they are before beginning cleanup so the spill is not left accessible to them.

A safer routine for entertaining and home brewing

Before guests arrive, decide where drinks will be placed and who clears them. Use a room the cat cannot enter for unattended serving trays, open punch bowls or brewing equipment. Collect half-finished glasses before going to bed instead of assuming the room is safe once people leave. Wipe spills promptly and keep the cat away while cleanup is in progress.

Remember that raw yeast dough can generate ethanol after ingestion, even when no beverage is involved. Proof dough and discard brewing or baking waste in inaccessible containers. Ask visitors to give only the cat's usual food if they want to offer a reward. A consistent no-drinks rule is easier to follow than making exceptions for a particular beer, a small glass or a cat that has appeared unaffected in the past.

What to do

Treat as an emergency and call your vet.
